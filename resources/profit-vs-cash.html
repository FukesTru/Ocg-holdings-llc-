Profit vs. Cash

Why Profitable Businesses Still Run Out of Cash

Guide 02 By Oscar Cancino, Founder · 6 min read

Every year, genuinely profitable businesses fail — not because the model was broken, but because the cash ran out before the profit arrived. If that sounds like a paradox, this guide is for you, because it means you're reading your P&L as if it were a bank statement. It isn't.

Profit Is an Opinion. Cash Is a Fact.

Your P&L is built on accrual accounting: revenue is recognized when it's earned, expenses when they're incurred. That's the right way to measure whether the business model works. But it deliberately ignores timing — and timing is the entire game of staying solvent.

  • You invoice a client $100K in March. The P&L says March was great. The cash arrives in May — if the client pays on time.
  • You buy $200K of inventory in June for Q4. The P&L barely notices — inventory isn't an expense until it sells. Your bank account noticed immediately.
  • Loan principal payments reduce your cash every month and never appear on the P&L at all.
The P&L answers "is this a good business?" The cash flow answers "will this business be alive in ninety days?" You need both answers, and they are not the same question.

Why Growth Makes It Worse

Here's the trap that catches strong businesses: growth consumes cash before it produces cash. To sell more next quarter, you buy more inventory, hire earlier, and extend terms to bigger customers — all cash out today. The corresponding cash in arrives one full cycle later.

The faster you grow, the wider that gap gets. This is why businesses so often hit their worst cash crisis in their best revenue year — and why "we'll grow our way out of it" usually deepens the hole it's trying to fill.

The Number That Explains It: Your Cash Conversion Cycle

Take three numbers most owners never compute:

  • Days Inventory Outstanding — how long product sits before it sells
  • Days Sales Outstanding — how long customers take to pay you
  • Days Payables Outstanding — how long you take to pay suppliers

Inventory days plus receivable days, minus payable days, is your cash conversion cycle — the number of days each dollar spends trapped inside your operations. If it's 75 days, every dollar of growth needs 75 days of financing from somewhere: your account, a credit line, or a crisis.

Shrinking that cycle is the cheapest capital raise available. It's why a 14-day DSO improvement — our client average — can matter more than a funding round.

How to See the Gap Before It Hurts

Three pieces of infrastructure close the blind spot:

  • A monthly close you trust. Accrual books, reconciled monthly — the foundation. (This is what our bookkeeping service exists for.)
  • A rolling 13-week cash forecast. The bridge between accrual reality and bank-account reality, refreshed weekly.
  • Someone accountable for reading both. A CFO layer that prices every big decision — the hire, the PO, the expansion — against the forecast before committing.

With those three in place, "profitable but broke" stops being a possible outcome, because the gap between profit and cash is visible months before it becomes a problem. Lenders notice too — businesses that can explain their cash conversion cycle are precisely the ones that get funded on good terms.

The Uncomfortable Question

Could you say, right now, whether you can make payroll in six weeks — not from memory, but from a document? If the answer is no, that's not a character flaw; it's a missing system. It's also fixable in about 30 days. A readiness assessment is where we'd start.
